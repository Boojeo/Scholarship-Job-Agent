In one run of my multi-agent experiment, a planner agent invented a Python package that does not exist. The implementer imported it, the reviewer approved it, and the final program failed. What stayed with me was not the error itself but how quietly it moved through three "intelligent" steps without anyone checking. That run became a 37,800-run study, my first first-author paper, and the question I want to keep working on in a master's degree: how do we make large language models efficient enough to use widely and reliable enough to trust?

Strong grades in linear algebra, probability and algorithms gave me the tools, but my direction came from KAUST Academy. I was selected among the top 100 of more than 17,000 applicants for its AI Specialization track, finished two 40-hour courses with distinction, and spent the summer of 2026 at KAUST in an eight-week programme covering deep learning, Transformers, reinforcement learning and LLM fine-tuning.

**Efficiency: reading more with less.** At KAUST I joined the team behind *Periscope*, a training-free method that lets a frozen language model read far beyond its context window by answering the same question over small local and strided spans of a long document. I worked on its retrieval side, first developed under the name CorpusProbe: with subquadratic cost, its evidence map reached NDCG@10 of 0.526 on BRIGHT, ahead of BM25 and embedding models. The paper is now under review at ICLR. Within the same work I built OBLIQ-Bench, which studies "oblique" queries: queries whose relevance to a document is hidden below the surface text. Measuring latency, cost and TFLOPs on KAUST's IBEX cluster and on T4 GPUs taught me that a method is only as useful as the compute it needs. I learned to treat runtime as a research result, not an afterthought.

**Reliability: checking the hand-offs.** Back at Najran, with Dr. Abdullah Khanfor, I designed the study I opened with. I injected seven types of false claims into a planner → implementer → reviewer pipeline across 150 tasks, three seeds and three open-weight model families. Unchecked hallucinations survived to the final code in up to 67% of runs. A simple deterministic tool check at each hand-off reduced surviving fake packages from 61% to 1.6%, while LLM-based supervision missed most wrong function signatures. I pre-registered the hypotheses, used task-clustered bootstrap confidence intervals, and re-ran all 2,844 runs in the reproducibility check with identical results, all on free GPU quotas. The lesson I took away is that careful experimental design matters more than large compute.

**Access: language that people actually speak.** My graduation project, *Lahja*, brings these ideas to Arabic dialects, where data is scarce and annotators are expensive. Using only free resources, I fine-tuned Jais with QLoRA for Emirati and Najdi Arabic, built an annotator-free pipeline from podcast speech, and designed a leakage-free evaluation suite. The model reached 82% preference for dialectal forms, against 55% for few-shot prompting, and its public demo has served 300 users.
